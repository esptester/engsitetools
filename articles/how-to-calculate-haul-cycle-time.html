How to calculate haul cycle time and loads per day

Cycle time drives everything in a haul: how many loads you get, how many trucks you need, and what each tonne costs. Here's how to work it out.

Published 6 November 2026 · 4 min read

What makes up a cycle

A truck's cycle is everything it does between one load and the next:

  • Spot: positioning at the loader
  • Load: the loader filling the truck
  • Haul: travelling loaded to the dump
  • Dump: manoeuvring and tipping
  • Return: travelling empty back to the loader
  • Queue: any time spent waiting for the loader

Travel time is distance divided by average speed: minutes = distance (km) ÷ speed (km/h) × 60. Use the average you'd expect over the whole run, not the truck's top speed. Accelerating, corners, grades, speed limits and the road surface all pull the average down.

Worked example: a dry day

A 2.4 km haul, one loader, and four trucks each carrying 25 t. The times and speeds are assumptions for the example:

  • Spot 0.5 min, load 4.0 min, dump 1.5 min
  • Haul loaded at an average of 25 km/h: 2.4 ÷ 25 × 60 = 5.76 min
  • Return empty at an average of 35 km/h: 2.4 ÷ 35 × 60 = 4.11 min

The cycle is 0.5 + 4.0 + 5.76 + 1.5 + 4.11 = 15.87 min.

From cycle time to loads per day

Nobody works a full 60 minutes in every hour. A common allowance is a 50-minute productive hour, which covers short stops, waiting and small delays. On that basis:

  • Loads per truck per hour: 50 ÷ 15.87 = 3.15
  • Over a 10-hour shift: 31.5 loads per truck
  • Four trucks could do 126 loads a day

Check the loader

The loader can only fill one truck at a time. Each truck spends 4.5 minutes at the loader (spot plus load), so the loader can manage at most 50 ÷ 4.5 = 11.1 loads an hour, or 111 loads in the 10-hour shift.

The trucks could do 126, but the loader caps it at 111. This fleet is loader-limited, and the trucks will spend some time queuing. A quick way to size the fleet is cycle time ÷ time at the loader: 15.87 ÷ 4.5 = 3.5 trucks keeps the loader busy. Three trucks would leave the loader waiting, at 94 loads a day. Four keep it working, with a little queuing.

A wet day

Rain on an unsealed haul road slows everything down. Say the averages drop to 18 km/h loaded and 25 km/h empty:

  • Haul 8.00 min and return 5.76 min, so the cycle becomes 19.76 min
  • Loads per truck: 50 ÷ 19.76 = 2.53 an hour, or 25.3 a shift
  • Four trucks: 101 loads a day

Now the loader could do 111 but the trucks only deliver 101. The fleet has become truck-limited, and production is down 9%. It would take 19.76 ÷ 4.5 = 4.4 trucks to keep the loader busy, so in the wet, a fifth truck would bring production back up to the loader's limit of 111 loads.

The numbers to get right

  • Average speeds for each road condition, from your own experience or site records
  • Time at the loader, which depends on the loader, the bucket and the material
  • Payload, limited by the truck's legal and rated capacity as well as its tray
  • Productive minutes per hour and hours per shift, after pre-starts, breaks and refuelling

The figures in this example are assumptions for the arithmetic. Use your own.
