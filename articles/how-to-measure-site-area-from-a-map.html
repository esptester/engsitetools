How to measure a site's area from a map

Map tools, survey coordinates and a bit of geometry will give you a site's area quickly. Here's how each method works, and the corrections that catch people out.

Published 6 November 2026 · 3 min read

Plan area, not surface area

A map shows the ground from directly above, so any area you measure on it is the plan area: the area projected onto a flat, horizontal surface. That's what lot sizes and most quantities use. On sloping ground the actual surface is bigger, which matters for anything laid on it, such as topsoil, turf, geofabric or erosion matting on a batter.

To convert, divide the plan area by the cosine of the slope angle:

Surface area = plan area ÷ cos(slope angle)

  • 5° slope: 0.4% more than the plan area
  • 10° slope: 1.5% more
  • 20° slope: 6.4% more
  • 30° slope: 15.5% more

Our guide to slope ratios covers converting a ratio like 1V:2H into an angle.

Method 1: trace it on a map

Online maps and apps let you place points around a boundary on aerial imagery and read off the area. It's fast, and good enough for planning and estimating. Zoom in when you place the corners, follow the real edge rather than shadows or tree canopy, and remember that imagery can sit a few metres away from its true position.

Method 2: coordinates and the shoelace formula

If you have coordinates for the corners, from a survey or a plan, you can calculate the area exactly with the shoelace formula. List the corners in order around the boundary. For each corner, multiply its x by the next corner's y, and subtract the next corner's x multiplied by its y. Add up the results and halve the total:

Area = ½ × | Σ (xi × yi+1 − xi+1 × yi) |

Here's an L-shaped yard with corners at (0, 0), (50, 0), (50, 20), (20, 20), (20, 40) and (0, 40), in metres:

FromTox × next y − next x × y
(0, 0)(50, 0)0 × 0 − 50 × 0 = 0
(50, 0)(50, 20)50 × 20 − 50 × 0 = 1,000
(50, 20)(20, 20)50 × 20 − 20 × 20 = 600
(20, 20)(20, 40)20 × 40 − 20 × 20 = 400
(20, 40)(0, 40)20 × 40 − 0 × 40 = 800
(0, 40)(0, 0)0 × 0 − 0 × 40 = 0

The total is 2,800, so the area is 2,800 ÷ 2 = 1,400 m². As a check, the L is a 50 × 20 rectangle plus a 20 × 20 one: 1,000 + 400 = 1,400 m².

Method 3: the lot's official area

For a whole lot, the area is shown on its survey plan, which you can get from your state's land titles office, such as Landgate in Western Australia. That's the figure to use for anything legal.

Method 4: walk the boundary with GPS

A phone can record a boundary as you walk it, but phone GPS is typically only accurate to a few metres, so use it for rough areas only.

Units

  • 1 hectare (ha) = 10,000 m²
  • 1 acre ≈ 4,046.9 m²
  • 1 ha ≈ 2.471 acres

The yard above is 1,400 m², which is 0.14 ha, or about 0.35 acres.

A note on accuracy

Map and app measurements are estimates for planning and quantities. Where the boundary or the exact area has legal or contractual weight, use a licensed surveyor.

Doing it on site

SiteArea measures plan area. You place points along the boundary on the map, and it gives the area as seen from above. It doesn't account for elevation, so on sloping ground, use the correction at the top of this article to get the surface area.
