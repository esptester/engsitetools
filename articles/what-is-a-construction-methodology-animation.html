What is a construction methodology animation?

A short 3D video that shows how you plan to build a job. Here's what one shows, when it helps a tender, and when it isn't worth the effort.

Published 6 November 2026 · 4 min read

The short answer

A construction methodology animation is a short 3D video, usually a minute or two long, that shows how a contractor plans to build a job. It walks through the stages in order: how the site is set up, where the plant goes, what gets built when, and how the site changes until the work is finished.

It takes the written methodology from a tender and turns it into something an assessor can watch in a couple of minutes, instead of piecing it together from pages of text and staging drawings.

What an animation shows

Most methodology animations cover some or all of these:

  • Site establishment: compounds, access points, fencing and laydown areas.
  • Staging and sequence: the order of work, stage by stage, and how each stage hands over to the next.
  • Plant and crews: the main equipment, where it sits and how it moves around the site.
  • Temporary works and traffic: the concept for things like shoring, traffic switches or staged closures.
  • Interfaces: how the work sits next to live traffic, existing services, neighbours or an operating facility.
  • The finished job: reinstatement and the completed works.

What it isn't

An animation illustrates the intent of a method. It is not proof that the method works. It is usually not to scale, the plant and people are generic models rather than the exact machines and crews you'll use, and the timing is compressed so that a month of work fits into a few seconds.

It is also not a design, an engineering check, a program or a safety document. Those stay in your tender as they are. The animation sits alongside them and helps the assessor understand them. If your tender needs the sequence tied to real program dates, that's a 4D simulation, which we compare in construction animation vs 4D BIM.

When it helps a tender

Methodology is often one of the main criteria a tender is scored on, alongside price and experience. An animation helps most when the method is the hard part to explain:

  • Complex staging: traffic switches, staged closures, or work that has to happen in a particular order.
  • Constrained or live sites: work next to traffic, occupied buildings, rail corridors or an operating plant, where how you protect the surroundings matters as much as the work itself.
  • Work that's hard to picture: anything underground, temporary works, or heavy lifts that drawings don't make obvious.
  • A method that's genuinely different: if your approach saves closures, time or risk compared with the obvious way, an animation makes the difference easy to see.
  • Projects with a public audience: the same video can explain the works to a client's board or to the local community.

When it isn't worth it

  • The work is simple and routine, and a one-page staging sketch tells the whole story.
  • The tender is decided on price alone, or doesn't accept video or extra attachments.
  • Your methodology isn't settled yet. Animating a method that's still changing wastes review rounds.
  • There isn't enough time before the closing date to review a draft properly.

How to use it in your submission

Start with the conditions of tendering. They set what you can submit, in what format, and whether material outside the returnable schedules is considered at all. Then:

  • Attach the video if the tender allows extra files, and check the portal's file size limit before you settle on a length and resolution.
  • Link to it from your written methodology if links are allowed, using a private or unlisted video link so it isn't public.
  • Use stills as figures in the written methodology, labelled as indicative, so the method still reads clearly if nobody presses play.
  • Keep it consistent with your written methodology, program and staging drawings. Assessors read them together, and a mismatch costs credibility.
  • Reuse it at the tender interview or presentation if you're shortlisted.

How long should it be?

Short. Assessors work through several submissions, and a video they finish is worth more than a longer one they skip through. One to three minutes covers most jobs. Spend the time on the stages that set your method apart, and move quickly through the parts every tenderer will do the same way.

What you'll need to provide

Mostly what you've already written for the tender: the methodology, staging plans, site drawings and a list of the stages in order. Our guide to briefing a construction animation has the full checklist.
